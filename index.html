MICRO · 1.1.1

Economics as a social science

How economists think: models, assumptions and why there's no lab.

Economics
The study of how scarce resources are allocated to satisfy unlimited wants. It is a social science because it studies human behaviour.
Economic model
A simplified representation of reality, built on assumptions, used to explain relationships between variables and make predictions (e.g. the PPF, demand and supply diagrams).

AThinking like an economist

  1. Observe a real-world problem or relationship (e.g. sugary drink consumption is rising).
  2. Build a model, simplifying reality using assumptions.
  3. Make a prediction / hypothesis (e.g. a sugar tax will cut consumption).
  4. Test it against real-world data.
  5. Refine the model if the evidence doesn't fit, then repeat.

Why make assumptions?

  • Reality is too complex: assumptions simplify so a model is usable.
  • They isolate the relationship between two variables (cause → effect).
  • They make predictions testable.

Common assumptions: agents are rational (consumers maximise utility, firms maximise profit), have perfect information, respond to incentives, and ceteris paribus.

BCeteris paribus

Ceteris paribus
"All other things being equal". Every variable is held constant except the one being studied, so its effect can be isolated.

Example: a rise in price causes a fall in quantity demanded, ceteris paribus. Without it, a simultaneous rise in income could mean demand rises anyway and the relationship gets hidden.

Exam tipWrite "ceteris paribus" whenever you explain a cause → effect chain in a model. It shows you know the model's limits.

CWhy economics can't run scientific experiments

No controlled lab conditions
In the real world you can't hold all other variables constant. Ceteris paribus is impossible in practice.
Can't repeat history
Every economic situation is unique. You can't rerun 2008 with a different interest rate.
Human behaviour is unpredictable
People aren't always rational, are influenced by others, and change behaviour over time.
Ethical limits
You can't deliberately make people unemployed or poor to test a theory.

So economists rely on historical data, natural experiments and statistical analysis. As a result, predictions are less certain than in natural sciences.

Case: the day-care fine (Gneezy & Rustichini, 2000)
A nursery fined parents who were late collecting their children. The model assumed people respond to negative incentives, so lateness should fall. In fact, lateness rose: parents treated the fine as a price for extra childcare, which removed their guilt. This shows that assumptions about behaviour can fail, and that's why models need testing.
Evaluation angleModels are unrealistic but still useful. They simplify complex reality enough to predict the direction of change, even if not the exact size.
MICRO · 1.1.2

Positive & normative statements

Fact vs opinion, and how value judgements drive policy.

Positive statement
An objective statement that can be tested / proved or disproved using evidence. It does not have to be true.
e.g. "A sugar tax will reduce consumption of sugary drinks."
Normative statement
A subjective statement based on opinion / value judgements. It cannot be tested.
e.g. "The government should introduce a sugar tax."
Exam traps
  • A false statement can still be positive: "UK unemployment is 50%" is wrong, but it's testable.
  • Normative signal words: should, ought, fair, unfair, better, too high, too low, unacceptable, most important.
  • Positive signal words: will, is, was, causes, leads to, plus numbers and data.
  • A normative statement can include a fact: "Inflation of 4% is too high" is still normative.

AValue judgements

Value judgement
A view about what is good or bad / right or wrong, based on personal beliefs and opinions rather than evidence.

Their role in decision-making and policy

  • Positive economics predicts the effects of a policy. Normative judgements then decide whether those effects are desirable.
  • They decide which objective to prioritise, e.g. low inflation vs low unemployment, or growth vs the environment.
  • Same evidence, different policies: raising the minimum wage helps low-paid workers (equity) but may cost some jobs. Whether that trade-off is "worth it" is a value judgement.
  • Policy is ultimately decided by politicians, whose values reflect their party and voters.
  • Even economists make value judgements in what they choose to study and how they interpret data, so analysis is never 100% objective.
Worked example: sugar tax (Soft Drinks Industry Levy, 2018)
Positive "Manufacturers cut sugar content in response to the levy." This is testable, and the evidence supports it.
Normative "The government is right to restrict consumer choice to improve public health." This is a value judgement about the role of the state.

The economic problem

Scarcity, resources and opportunity cost.

Scarcity / the economic problem
Resources are finite but wants are unlimited, so choices must be made about how resources are allocated.

Every economy must answer: What to produce? How to produce it? For whom?

AFactors of production (CELL)

FactorWhat it isReward
CapitalMan-made goods used to produce other goods (machinery, factories, roads)Interest
EnterpriseOrganises the other factors and takes the riskProfit
LandNatural resources (land, oil, fish, minerals)Rent
LabourHuman effort, physical and mentalWages

BRenewable vs non-renewable resources

Renewable
Can be replenished, so stocks can be maintained if used sustainably. e.g. wind, solar, timber, fish stocks.
Non-renewable
Finite: once used, they cannot be replaced. e.g. oil, coal, natural gas, metals.
Watch outA renewable resource becomes effectively non-renewable if used faster than it regenerates, e.g. overfishing (North Sea cod) or deforestation. That's why sustainability matters.

COpportunity cost

Opportunity cost
The value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.

It exists because of scarcity: choosing one thing means giving up another. That's why "there's no such thing as a free lunch". Free school meals still use scarce resources that could have gone elsewhere.

Consumers

Limited income and time. £60 on a game = trainers forgone. An evening revising = wages from a shift forgone.

Producers

Limited capital, labour, factory space. Using a factory line for product A means not making B. Reinvesting profit means lower dividends.

Government

Limited tax revenue. £10bn on the NHS = £10bn less for education or defence, or a tax cut forgone.

Why it matters: it forces agents to weigh up trade-offs, so resources go to their most valued use. Governments use it to compare the benefits of spending options.

Production possibility frontiers

The most-tested diagram in Theme 1. Know every point and every shift.

PPF
A curve showing the maximum possible combinations of two goods an economy can produce when all resources are fully and efficiently employed, given current technology.
PPF labInteractive

AReading a PPF

Producing at any point on the PPF: max output from resources, no waste.
Allocative efficiency
The one point on the PPF that best matches society's preferences, so resources produce the mix of goods society wants most.

BOpportunity cost & marginal analysis

Moving along the PPF: to produce more of one good, resources must be taken from the other. The OC is the amount of the other good given up.

Concave (bowed out) PPF → increasing OC

Resources are not equally suited to producing both goods (imperfect factor substitutability). Shift more into food and you start using resources that are good at clothes but poor at food, so you lose more clothes per extra unit of food.

Straight-line PPF → constant OC

Resources are perfectly substitutable between the two goods, so every extra unit of X always costs the same amount of Y.

Why "marginal"?Decisions are made one unit at a time. Agents compare the extra benefit with the extra cost of the next unit, not the total.

Causes of an outward shift (↑ quantity or quality of CELL)

  • Investment in capital goods (machinery, infrastructure)
  • New technology / better production methods
  • Education & training → better quality labour (human capital)
  • Larger labour force: net immigration, higher birth rate, more people entering work
  • Discovery of new resources (e.g. North Sea oil)
  • Specialisation / division of labour → higher productivity

DCapital goods vs consumer goods

Consumer goods
Goods bought to satisfy wants directly (food, phones, clothes).
Capital goods
Goods used to produce other goods (machinery, factories, roads). Creating them is investment.
  1. Country A puts more resources into capital goods today, so fewer consumer goods are produced.
  2. Opportunity cost = consumption and living standards today.
  3. More capital → higher productivity → PPF shifts out further in future.
  4. A can consume more of both goods in the long run than B. It's a trade-off between now and the future.
MICRO · 1.1.5

Specialisation & the division of labour

Adam Smith, pros and cons for firms and countries, and the functions of money.

Specialisation
When an individual, firm, region or country concentrates on producing a limited range of goods or services.
Division of labour
Breaking a production process into separate tasks, with each worker doing one task. It's specialisation at the level of the individual worker.
Adam Smith: The Wealth of Nations (1776), the pin factory
One worker doing every step "could scarce make one pin a day, and certainly not twenty". Splitting pin-making into ~18 distinct operations let 10 workers make ~48,000 pins a day (≈4,800 each). Smith argued the division of labour is the key source of productivity, growth and the "wealth of nations".

ADivision of labour in organising production

Advantages
  • Practice makes perfect: repetition → skill, speed, accuracy → ↑ productivity (output per worker per hour).
  • No time lost switching tasks or tools.
  • Simple tasks are easier to mechanise / automate.
  • Lower training costs: each worker learns one task, and recruitment is easier.
  • Workers can be matched to the tasks they're best at.
  • ↑ output → lower average costs → lower prices / higher profits, more competitive.
Disadvantages
  • Boredom / alienation from repetition → lower motivation, mistakes, absenteeism, higher labour turnover → productivity can fall.
  • Interdependence: one absent worker, strike or broken machine can halt the whole line.
  • Narrow skills → occupational immobility → structural unemployment if the task is automated.
  • Standardised products: less variety, loss of craft skills.

BSpecialising in production to trade (countries)

Advantages
  • Countries specialise where they have the lowest opportunity cost, so world output rises (combined PPFs effectively shift out).
  • Economies of scale: bigger markets → lower average costs → lower prices.
  • More choice and variety for consumers via imports.
  • Competition → efficiency and innovation.
  • Access to goods and resources a country can't produce (e.g. UK imports bananas, oil).
Disadvantages
  • Over-dependence on imports → vulnerable to supply shocks (e.g. Europe's reliance on Russian gas in 2022, PPE shortages in COVID).
  • Vulnerable to a fall in world demand/price for the specialised good (economies reliant on one commodity like oil).
  • Structural unemployment when industries decline due to foreign competition (UK coal, steel).
  • Trade barriers or exchange-rate swings can block the gains.
  • Transport costs and environmental damage from shipping.

CFunctions of money

Specialisation only works if people can trade surpluses. Under barter you need a double coincidence of wants (each side must want what the other has), which is inefficient. Money solves this.

Medium of exchange
Generally accepted in exchange for goods and services. Removes the need for a double coincidence of wants.
Measure of value (unit of account)
Values of different goods can be expressed and compared in one common unit (prices).
Store of value
Holds purchasing power over time, so it can be saved and spent later. (Weakened by inflation.)
Method of deferred payment
Allows debts to be settled in the future in an agreed money amount (loans, credit).
MnemonicMedium · Measure · Store · Deferred: "My Money Stays Deposited".
MICRO · 1.2.1

Rational decision making

The assumptions behind demand and supply.

Rational economic decision making
Economic agents use available information to make choices that maximise their own benefit, weighing up costs and benefits at the margin.
CONSUMERS

Aim to maximise utility

Utility = satisfaction from consuming a good or service. With a limited income, consumers allocate spending to get the highest total utility.

They keep buying while the extra satisfaction (marginal utility) of the next unit is worth at least its price. They spread income so the last £ spent on each good gives equal marginal utility.

FIRMS

Aim to maximise profit

Profit = Total Revenue − Total Cost

Firms produce what consumers want at the lowest cost, and expand output as long as each extra unit adds more to revenue than to cost.

Why assume it? It makes behaviour predictable, so we can model it: higher price → consumers buy less (law of demand), firms supply more (law of supply).

Evaluation: is it realistic?
  • Imperfect information: consumers rarely know all prices and options.
  • Bounded rationality: limited time and brainpower, so people use rules of thumb.
  • Habits and herd behaviour: people copy others, or stick with defaults and the status quo.
  • Firms may pursue other goals: sales or revenue growth, survival, market share, social responsibility, or satisficing (making "enough" profit).

Demand
The quantity of a good or service consumers are willing and able to buy at a given price over a given time period (effective demand).
Law of demand
An inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded: as price falls, quantity demanded rises, ceteris paribus.

CDiminishing marginal utility → downward-sloping demand

Total utility (TU)
The total satisfaction from consuming all units of a good.
Marginal utility (MU)
The extra satisfaction from consuming one more unit.
Law of diminishing marginal utility
As more units of a good are consumed, the marginal utility from each additional unit falls.

  • While MU is positive, TU rises (even though MU is falling).
  • TU is at its maximum when MU = 0 (point of satiety).
  • MU negative → TU falls (disutility). A rational consumer stops before this, even if the good is free.

The link to the demand curve (learn this chain)

  1. Each extra unit gives less extra satisfaction (diminishing MU).
  2. So consumers are willing to pay less for each additional unit.
  3. A rational consumer buys a unit only if its MU ≥ price.
  4. So they will only buy more if price falls, which gives a downward-sloping demand curve.

Other reasons demand slopes down: income effect (price ↓ → real income / purchasing power ↑ → can buy more) and substitution effect (price ↓ → good is relatively cheaper than substitutes → consumers switch to it).

Evaluation: exceptionsVeblen goods (luxury or status goods like designer handbags) can see demand rise as price rises, because a high price signals exclusivity. Also speculative demand: rising house or share prices can increase demand if people expect further rises.
MICRO · 1.2.4

Supply

Movements vs shifts and the conditions of supply.

Supply
The quantity of a good or service producers are willing and able to sell at a given price over a given time period.
Law of supply
A positive relationship between price and quantity supplied: as price rises, quantity supplied rises, ceteris paribus.

Why the supply curve slopes upwards

  1. Price ↑ → producing the good becomes more profitable (profit motive).
  2. Existing firms expand output, and higher prices cover the rising cost of producing extra units.
  3. New firms are attracted into the market, and firms switch resources from less profitable products.
  4. Quantity supplied ↑ → extension along the supply curve.

MACRO · 2.1.1

Economic growth

GDP, real vs nominal, GNI, PPP, limitations and happiness.

Economic growth (short run / actual)
An increase in real GDP over time, measured as the % change in real GDP (usually per year or per quarter).
Gross Domestic Product (GDP)
The total value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders over a period of time (usually a year).

GDP can be measured by output, income or expenditure, which are equal in theory. Only final goods count (no double-counting of intermediate goods), and second-hand sales don't count.

Growth rate (%) = (Real GDPthis yr − Real GDPlast yr) ÷ Real GDPlast yr × 100
Recession
Two consecutive quarters of negative real GDP growth.

AReal vs nominal · total vs per capita · value vs volume

MeasureDefinitionUse
Nominal GDPGDP at current prices. Not adjusted for inflation.Can rise just because prices rise.
Real GDPGDP at constant prices, adjusted for inflation.Shows true change in output. Growth is always measured in real terms.
Total GDPWhole economy's output.Size of the economy.
GDP per capitaGDP ÷ population.Average income per person, a better guide to living standards.

Real GDP = Nominal GDP × (100 ÷ price index)   [base year index = 100]
Quick approx: real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation
Exam traps
  • If population grows faster than real GDP, GDP per capita falls even though GDP rises.
  • A fall in the growth rate (3% → 1%) means GDP is still rising, just more slowly. Only negative growth means GDP is falling.
  • Nominal GDP can rise while real GDP falls (high inflation).

BGross National Income (GNI)

GNI
GDP + net income from abroad: income earned by a country's residents from overseas assets and work (profits, interest, dividends, wages), minus income paid out to foreign owners of domestic assets.
GNI = GDP + (income received from abroad − income paid abroad)
  • GNI > GDP when residents own many assets abroad, or many citizens work abroad and send money home.
  • GNI < GDP when a lot of domestic output is foreign-owned and profits flow out. Ireland is the classic example: multinationals book huge profits there, inflating GDP well above what residents actually receive.
  • GNI is a better measure of the income actually available to a country's residents.

CComparing growth between countries and over time

  • Use real figures to remove inflation, per capita to remove population differences, and PPP to remove exchange-rate and cost-of-living differences.
  • Base effect: poorer countries often grow faster in % terms from a low base (catch-up). 3% growth on a large GDP can be a bigger absolute increase than 7% on a small one.
  • Look at trends over several years, not one year, since a single year can be distorted by a shock (e.g. the 2020 COVID collapse, then the rebound in 2021).

Index numbers

Index = (value in year ÷ value in base year) × 100   base year = 100
The classic trapAn index rising from 120 to 132 is a 10% increase ((132−120)/120 × 100), not 12%. You can only read the change straight off when moving from the base year (100). Also: a percentage-point change (2% → 3% = +1pp) is not the same as a percentage change (+50%).

DPurchasing power parity (PPP)

Purchasing power parity
An exchange rate that equalises the purchasing power of two currencies: the rate at which the same basket of goods costs the same in both countries. It adjusts GDP for differences in the cost of living.

Why not just use market exchange rates?

  • Market rates are volatile. They move with speculation and interest rates, so GDP in $ changes even if output doesn't.
  • They reflect traded goods only and ignore the much lower prices of non-traded goods and services (haircuts, rent, food) in poorer countries. So they understate living standards in low-income countries.

Effect: PPP-adjusted figures narrow the gap between rich and poor countries. In the Theme 2 book data, Norway's GDP per capita was ~65× India's at market rates but only ~17× using PPP.

Big Mac index (The Economist): if a Big Mac costs ¥200 in Tokyo and $2 in New York, the PPP rate is $1 = ¥100.

ELimitations of GDP to compare living standards

LimitationWhy it matters
InequalityGDP per capita is an average. It hides how income is distributed, so a few very rich people can raise it while most are poor.
Hidden / informal economyCash-in-hand work and illegal activity go unrecorded, so GDP is understated (especially in developing countries).
Non-market outputSubsistence farming, housework, childcare, DIY and volunteering aren't counted. GDP also rises when these move into the market (e.g. more women in paid work, paying for childcare) even if output hasn't changed.

Environment / externalitiesPollution and resource depletion aren't subtracted. Growth can lower quality of life.

Health, education, happinessGDP counts spending on healthcare and education, not actual outcomes like life expectancy or literacy.

FNational happiness & wellbeing

UK national wellbeing (ONS, since 2010)

The ONS Measuring National Wellbeing programme looks beyond GDP. It asks four personal wellbeing questions, each scored 0–10:

1. Life satisfaction: how satisfied are you with your life nowadays?
2. Worthwhile: to what extent do you feel the things you do in your life are worthwhile?
3. Happiness: how happy did you feel yesterday?
4. Anxiety: how anxious did you feel yesterday?

It also tracks wider domains: health, relationships, what we do (work/leisure), where we live, personal finance, the economy, education & skills, governance and the environment.

Real incomes and subjective happiness

Easterlin paradox (1974)
Within a country, richer people are happier than poorer people. But over time, as countries get richer, average happiness does not rise much.
  • Diminishing marginal utility of income: at low incomes, extra money buys essentials (food, shelter, healthcare), so happiness rises a lot. At high incomes, each extra £ adds little.
  • Relative income matters: people compare themselves with others. If everyone gets richer, nobody feels better off ("keeping up with the Joneses").
  • Adaptation: people get used to higher living standards, and their expectations rise.
  • Happiness depends on non-income factors: health, relationships, job security, freedom, community. Growth can also bring longer hours, stress and pollution.
  • Kahneman & Deaton (2010): day-to-day emotional wellbeing in the US rose with income only up to ~$75,000 a year.
EvaluationLater studies (e.g. Stevenson & Wolfers, and more recent US data) find happiness keeps rising with income if measured in % terms, and richer countries are on average happier. Also, happiness is self-reported and subjective, so it's hard to compare across people and cultures.
MACRO · 2.1.2 a–d

Inflation

Definitions, calculating CPI, its limitations, and RPI. Causes and effects aren't on your test.

Inflation
A sustained increase in the general price level over time, which means a fall in the purchasing power of money.
Deflation
A sustained fall in the general price level (a negative inflation rate).
Disinflation
A fall in the rate of inflation. Prices are still rising, just more slowly (e.g. 6% → 3%).
The trap they always setInflation falling from 4% to 2% is disinflation, not deflation. Prices are still 2% higher than a year ago. Deflation only happens when the rate goes below 0%. Also: a one-off price rise isn't inflation, because it must be sustained and in the general price level.

UK target: CPI inflation of 2%, set by the Government and pursued by the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee. If inflation is more than 1 percentage point away (above 3% or below 1%), the Governor must write an open letter to the Chancellor explaining why. (UK CPI peaked at 11.1% in Oct 2022.)

AHow CPI is calculated

  1. Living Costs and Food Survey: thousands of households record their spending. This decides what goes in the basket (~700 representative goods and services) and each item's weight.
  2. Weights = the proportion of household spending on each item (bigger share → bigger weight). The basket and weights are updated every year to reflect changing spending (new items in, outdated ones out).
  3. Price collection: each month, prices of every item are collected from shops across the UK and online.
  4. Each item gets a price index relative to the base year (= 100). CPI averages prices using a geometric mean.
  5. Multiply each price index by its weight, add them up and divide by the total weights. That gives the weighted price index (CPI).
  6. Inflation rate = % change in CPI compared with the same month a year earlier.
Weighted index = Σ(price index × weight) ÷ Σ weights
Inflation rate = (CPInow − CPIyear ago) ÷ CPIyear ago × 100
Exam tipWeights might be given out of 100 or out of 1000 (the ONS uses parts per 1000). Either way, divide by the total of the weights.

Substitution biasWhen a good's price rises, consumers switch to cheaper alternatives. A fixed basket doesn't fully capture this, so CPI overstates inflation.
New products enter lateItems only join the basket once widely bought, and the basket is only updated annually.
Survey / sampling errorsThe LCFS relies on households accurately recording spending. Very rich and very poor households are under-represented.

Retail Prices Index (RPI)
An older measure of inflation (since 1947) that includes housing costs such as mortgage interest payments and council tax, and is calculated using an arithmetic mean.

MACRO · 2.1.3 a–c, e

Employment & unemployment

Measures, underemployment, the three rates, and migration & skills. Causes (d) and effects (f) aren't on your test.

Unemployment
People of working age who are willing and able to work and actively seeking work, but cannot find a job.

AMeasuring unemployment

MEASURE 1

Claimant count

The number of people claiming unemployment-related benefits: Jobseeker's Allowance + Universal Credit claimants who are required to look for work. Published monthly from administrative data.

+ Cheap, quick, monthly, detailed regional data, no sampling error.
− Eligibility rules change often (≈30 times since 1979), so comparisons over time are hard and can be politically manipulated.
− Excludes jobless people who aren't eligible (partner's income or savings too high, most under-18s), or who choose not to claim (stigma, unaware).
− Some claimants work illegally in the hidden economy. Universal Credit also brings in some low-paid workers who must search for more hours, which pushes the count up.

MEASURE 2

ILO / Labour Force Survey

A large quarterly household survey using the International Labour Organisation definition. Unemployed = aged 16+, without a job, actively sought work in the past 4 weeks, and available to start within 2 weeks (or has found a job and is waiting to start within 2 weeks).

+ Internationally agreed definition, so it allows cross-country comparison.
+ Includes people who can't claim benefits, and isn't affected by benefit rule changes.
− Sampling error. It's a survey, and falling response rates have reduced its reliability in recent years.
− Expensive and slower to publish. Relies on honest answers.
− Anyone doing 1+ hour of paid work a week counts as employed, and discouraged workers count as inactive, so it can understate joblessness.

Why is ILO usually higher than the claimant count?The ILO measure includes jobless people who are not eligible for benefits or choose not to claim: e.g. people whose partner earns too much, people with savings, 16–17 year-olds, students looking for work, and people seeking part-time work.

BUnemployment vs underemployment

Underemployment
People who are in work but are not working to their full capacity: either working fewer hours than they want (part-time but want full-time) or in jobs that don't use their skills and qualifications.
  • Time-related: a part-timer who wants full-time hours, or someone on a zero-hours contract.
  • Skills-related: an engineering graduate working as a delivery driver.
  • The underemployed count as employed in both measures, so headline unemployment understates spare capacity in the labour market.
  • It means wasted resources (the economy is operating inside its PPF), lower incomes and lower tax revenue.

CEmployment, unemployment & inactivity rates

Employed
Have a job (incl. part-time, self-employed).
Unemployed
No job, actively looking, available to start.
Economically inactive
Not in work and not looking or not available: students, early retirees, long-term sick, carers, discouraged workers.

Economically active (the labour force) = employed + unemployed. Working-age population (16–64) = active + inactive.

Employment rate = employed ÷ working-age population × 100
Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ economically active × 100
Inactivity rate = inactive ÷ working-age population × 100
Exam trap: different denominatorsThe unemployment rate divides by the economically active, not the whole working-age population. So employment and unemployment rates can both rise at once, and they don't add up to 100%.
Labour market calculatorInteractive
Book example: 100 working-age people (40 employed, 10 unemployed, 50 inactive). Try the scenarios.

Significance of changes in the rates

ChangeInterpretation
Unemployment rate ↓Usually good, but check why. If people stop looking (discouraged → inactive), the rate falls without anyone finding work. Always look at the employment rate alongside it.
Employment rate ↑More people working → higher incomes, consumer spending and tax revenue, lower benefit spending, higher output.
Employment and unemployment ↑ togetherThe labour force is growing: migrants, or inactive people (students, carers) starting to look for work.
Inactivity rate ↑Smaller labour force → lower productive potential (PPF shifts in) and labour shortages that push up wages. Also lower tax revenue and higher welfare spending. UK inactivity rose after COVID, driven by long-term sickness.
Inactivity ↓More potential workers. Unemployment may rise at first as they begin searching.

DThe significance of migration and skills

Skills

  • Skills mismatch: workers' skills don't match what employers need, so vacancies and unemployment exist at the same time (structural unemployment). Examples: the decline of coal and manufacturing, automation and AI replacing routine jobs.
  • Occupational immobility: workers can't easily move between jobs because they lack transferable skills or qualifications.
  • Higher-skilled workers are more employable, more adaptable and more productive. Low-skilled workers face higher unemployment and are hit first in downturns.
  • Skills shortages (e.g. HGV drivers in 2021, nurses, construction) leave vacancies unfilled and limit how much firms can produce.
  • The longer someone is unemployed, the more their skills erode, so it gets harder to find work (long-term unemployment).
  • Geographical immobility: high housing costs or family ties stop people moving to where the jobs are, causing regional unemployment.

Migration

Can reduce unemployment / boost employment
  • Net inward migration increases the labour force, which raises productive potential (PPF shifts out).
  • Migrants often fill skills shortages (NHS doctors and nurses, care workers, seasonal farm work). That eases bottlenecks so firms can expand, which can create jobs for domestic workers too.
  • Migrants are also consumers. Their spending raises demand, and so jobs. The number of jobs isn't fixed (the "lump of labour" fallacy).
  • Migrants are often working age, which offsets an ageing population and lowers inactivity.
Can raise unemployment / other concerns
  • The effect on the unemployment rate depends on whether migrants find work. If many are unemployed, the rate rises (try the calculator).
  • Possible competition for low-skilled jobs and downward pressure on wages in some sectors or areas. Evidence suggests the overall effect is small.
  • Easy access to migrant labour may reduce firms' incentive to train domestic workers.
  • Emigration of skilled workers ("brain drain") causes skills shortages in the country they leave.

UK context: since 2021 (post-Brexit), a points-based immigration system prioritises skills and salary thresholds, with specific shortage-occupation routes such as health and care.

PRACTICE · EXAM TECHNIQUE

Exam technique

How the marks are actually given, plus model plans to copy.

ADefinitions (1–2 marks)

Precision earns the marks. Learn the exact wording, using the key words in bold on every definition card. Vague answers ("inflation is when prices go up") lose a mark. Write: "a sustained increase in the general price level".

BSupported multiple choice (Edexcel style)

  • 1 mark for the correct letter, plus up to 3 marks for the explanation.
  • Explanation marks come from: defining the key term, applying the data or drawing the diagram, and rejecting wrong options (e.g. "B is wrong because a fall in the inflation rate is disinflation, not deflation").
  • Rejection marks still count if you pick the wrong answer, so always explain.

CThe macro 5-marker ("Explain…")

Marks are typically split across Knowledge (definition), Application (use the data or context) and Analysis (a developed chain of reasoning). No evaluation is needed.

  1. Define the key term(s) precisely.
  2. Apply: quote or calculate from the data (with units), or use the context given.
  3. Analyse: explain the point with a because → so → therefore chain. For "explain two…", give two separate, developed points.
Model: "Explain the difference between real and nominal GDP." (5)

K: Nominal GDP is the value of output at current prices, not adjusted for inflation. Real GDP is measured at constant prices, adjusted for inflation, so it shows changes in the volume of output.

A: e.g. if nominal GDP rises from £2,000bn to £2,100bn (5%) while inflation is 3%, real GDP has only grown by roughly 2%.

An: Nominal growth can be driven purely by rising prices, with no extra goods or services produced. So real GDP is used to measure economic growth and changes in living standards. Higher nominal GDP doesn't mean people can consume more.

Model: "Explain two limitations of using CPI to measure inflation." (5)

K: CPI is a weighted average of the prices of a basket of ~700 goods and services, with weights from the Living Costs and Food Survey.

Limitation 1 (An): CPI excludes owner-occupier housing costs such as mortgage interest and council tax. For homeowners with mortgages, when interest rates rise their cost of living rises faster than CPI shows, so CPI understates the inflation they face.

Limitation 2 (An): There is no "average" household. Low-income and pensioner households spend a larger share of income on food and energy. When those prices surged (e.g. 2022), their personal inflation rate was above CPI, so CPI misrepresents their loss of purchasing power.

Model: "Explain why the ILO measure of unemployment is usually higher than the claimant count." (5)

K: The claimant count measures people claiming unemployment-related benefits (JSA / Universal Credit, required to seek work). The ILO measure, from the Labour Force Survey, counts people without a job who have looked for work in the past 4 weeks and can start within 2 weeks.

An: Many unemployed people are not eligible to claim, e.g. if their partner's income or their savings are too high, or they're 16–17. Others choose not to claim because of stigma or not knowing they're entitled. These people are counted by the ILO survey but not by the claimant count.

A: Use any figures given, e.g. "ILO unemployment of 1.6m vs a claimant count of 1.2m, a gap of 0.4m."

Model: "Explain why GDP per capita may not accurately compare living standards between two countries." (5)

K: GDP per capita = total value of output ÷ population, i.e. average income per person.

An 1: It's an average, so it ignores inequality. A country with high GDP per capita may have most income going to a small elite, so the typical person's living standard is lower than the figure suggests.

An 2: Converting at market exchange rates ignores cost-of-living differences. Prices of non-traded goods are much lower in poorer countries, so their living standards are understated. PPP-adjusted figures should be used.

DThe micro 10-marker ("Examine…")

KAA: 6 marks

Knowledge, Application, Analysis. Define terms, use the context, draw a labelled diagram where relevant, and build 2 developed chains of reasoning.

Evaluation: 4 marks

Make 2 developed evaluation points that challenge or qualify your analysis, then give a short justified judgement.

Structure (~15 min): Definition → KAA point 1 (+ diagram) → KAA point 2 → Evaluation 1 → Evaluation 2 → Judgement.

Evaluation starters that always work
  • It depends on… (size of the change, type of good/firm, whether other factors stay constant)
  • Short run vs long run: effects take time, or fade
  • Magnitude: how big is the effect really? Use the data
  • Ceteris paribus rarely holds: other factors may offset it
  • Stakeholders: good for firms, bad for workers? Consumers vs producers
  • Prioritise: which point matters most, and why

Model plans: try writing each yourself before opening

"Examine the advantages and disadvantages to a car manufacturer of using the division of labour." (10)

Define: division of labour = splitting production into separate tasks, each done by a different worker (Adam Smith's pin factory: 10 workers, ~48,000 pins/day).

KAA 1: Workers repeat one task (e.g. fitting doors) → become more skilled and faster, with no time lost switching tasks → output per worker rises → average cost per car falls → the firm can lower prices or raise profit margins and become more competitive.

KAA 2: Simple tasks are easier to mechanise → robots on the production line → further productivity gains. Training is quicker and cheaper since workers only learn one task.

Eval 1: Repetition → boredom and alienation → lower motivation, more errors and higher absenteeism and labour turnover. This could offset productivity gains, especially in the long run.

Eval 2: Interdependence: one breakdown, absent worker or strike at a single stage halts the whole line. That's costly for a high-volume car plant, since lost output can't easily be recovered.

Judgement: The advantages likely dominate for a mass-production firm like a car maker, where economies of scale matter most. But the net benefit depends on managing motivation (e.g. job rotation) and building resilience into the line.

"Examine the likely effects on an economy of increasing its production of capital goods." (10)

Define: capital goods are goods used to produce other goods (machinery, infrastructure). Consumer goods satisfy wants directly. Diagram: a PPF of capital vs consumer goods. Show a movement along it towards capital goods, then a larger outward shift in future.

KAA 1: In the short run, resources move from consumer to capital goods (a movement along the PPF). The opportunity cost is consumer goods forgone, so current living standards fall.

KAA 2: More capital → higher labour productivity → productive capacity rises → PPF shifts outwards (potential growth) → more of both goods can be produced in future, raising long-run living standards.

Eval 1: It depends on the quality of investment. Badly chosen projects (e.g. unused infrastructure) won't raise productivity much, and some investment only replaces depreciating capital rather than adding to it.

Eval 2: Time lags: the benefits take years to arrive, while the sacrifice is immediate. That's harder for poorer economies, where cutting consumption may push people below subsistence. It also needs workers with the skills to use the new capital.

Judgement: Beneficial in the long run if the investment is productive. The size of the gain depends on the type of capital and how well complementary factors (skills, technology) keep up.

"Examine two factors that might lead to an increase in demand for electric cars." (10)

Define: demand = quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at each price. An increase in demand = the curve shifts right. Diagram: D₁ → D₂, showing a higher quantity demanded at every price.

KAA 1: Rising incomes. Electric cars are normal goods (arguably luxuries). Higher real incomes → more consumers able to afford them → demand shifts right.

KAA 2: Price of a related good. A rise in the price of petrol (a complement to petrol cars, which are substitutes for EVs) → petrol cars become more expensive to run → consumers switch to EVs → demand shifts right. Also: changing tastes, as environmental concern rises.

Eval 1: Size of the shift depends on how big the income rise is, and whether other factors are holding demand back, such as a lack of charging points (a complement), which could limit the shift.

Eval 2: Ceteris paribus rarely holds. Rising interest rates make car finance more expensive, which could offset higher incomes. Expectations of future price falls (as battery tech improves) may also make consumers delay buying.

Judgement: Petrol prices and tastes probably matter more than income in the short run. The biggest long-run driver may be complementary infrastructure (charging points).

"Examine the costs and benefits to a country of specialising and trading." (10)

Define: specialisation = concentrating on a narrow range of goods and services, then trading surpluses for others.

KAA 1: Specialise where opportunity cost is lowest → resources are used more efficiently → total output rises → trade lets the country consume beyond its own PPF → higher living standards.

KAA 2: A bigger export market → economies of scale → lower average costs → lower prices for consumers and greater competitiveness. Consumers also gain variety from imports.

Eval 1: Over-dependence: a country specialising in one commodity (e.g. oil) is vulnerable to a collapse in world prices. Reliance on imports makes it vulnerable to supply shocks (Europe's dependence on Russian gas, 2022).

Eval 2: Structural unemployment: industries that can't compete decline (UK coal and steel), and workers with narrow skills struggle to find new jobs. Gains also depend on low trade barriers and transport costs.

Judgement: Net benefits are usually positive, but greatest for countries that diversify across several specialisms and support workers to retrain.

"Examine factors that may cause the supply of wheat to decrease." (10)

Define: supply = quantity producers are willing and able to sell at each price. A decrease = the curve shifts left. Diagram: S₁ → S₂ (leftward).

KAA 1: Weather. Drought or flooding → smaller harvest → less wheat available at every price → supply shifts left (e.g. 2018 European drought).

KAA 2: Rising costs of production. Higher fertiliser and energy prices (gas is a key fertiliser input, and its price surged in 2022) → each tonne costs more to produce → lower profit at each price → farmers supply less → shift left. An indirect tax would work the same way.

Eval 1: Magnitude and time: weather shocks are often temporary (one season), while cost increases may persist. Farmers can also store wheat to smooth supply.

Eval 2: Offsetting factors: technology (drought-resistant seeds) or government subsidies could shift supply back right. Global supply also depends on many countries, so a local shock may matter less.

Judgement: Weather causes the largest short-run shifts, but sustained cost increases matter most over time.
